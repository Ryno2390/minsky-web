The Enterprise Margin

A monetary policy model built on Shaikh's principle that what drives accumulation is the profit rate net of interest — what happened when eighty years of US national accounts were allowed to answer back, and what was salvaged by going to a different part of the same book.

read first

This page is in two parts, and the first one fails. Part one (Results 1–6) builds a model of Shaikh's enterprise-margin mechanism, and Result 7 confronts it with the national accounts, where it does not survive: the headline elasticity dg/dm = 0.95 was measured at a leverage and an interest burden the US corporate sector has never approached, the constraint it describes has never once bound, and the interest deduction it turns on carries no aggregate information.

Part two (Results 8–12) goes back to the same chapter for a different argument — Shaikh's theory of the interest rate — and that one holds up better. It derives what part one assumed, it fits the Treasury curve once repaired, and it gives a policy rule worth racing. Along the way it also overturns two measurements of my own and one prediction I made a section earlier.

The argument

The profit rate is not what a firm gets to keep. The creditor is paid first, and net investment comes out of what is left — Shaikh's rate of profit of enterprise:

rE = r − iL·d r pre-interest profit rate, Π/pK iL the rate firms borrow at d leverage, L/pK

If accumulation keys off rE rather than r, monetary policy has an observable anchor — which gives two rules to compare:

SHAIKH ip = r − m keyed to the profit rate TAYLOR ip = r* + π + aπ(π − π*) + aU(u − u*) keyed to inflation and slack

Shaikh's second condition is that banking is a business, so competition equalises its profit rate with the general rate. That pins the spread rather than the level, and it is written as a tendency because that is the actual claim — rates are equalised by capital moving in and out, always overshooting:

rB = (iL·L − iD·DH − OpC) / EB d(spread)/dt = ths·(r − rB)

The two rules are calibrated to agree at the baseline, so any later difference between them is a difference in how they respond, not in where they start.

The accounting is the test

Three Godley tables — banks, firms, households — sharing the stocks they hold against each other. Every payment appears twice and every row nets to zero. Real capital is deliberately not in a table: it is not a claim on anybody, so it has no counterparty and no place on a balance sheet of claims.

Two identities fall out of writing the flows down consistently. Neither is imposed anywhere, which is what makes them worth checking:

That is not a failure of the mechanism — it is what a labour-constrained growth model must say. Accumulation has to equal α+β, and the investment function then forces rE = rEnorm. Neither is free.

A tighter margin does not slow accumulation. It requires a higher profit rate, and a higher level of activity, to sustain the same accumulation.

The burden falls on distribution, not on growth. The growth statement survives where growth is accumulation-determined rather than labour-determined — the core and rungs 1 and 2, which have no labour force, where dg*/dm = κ·d* = 0.95 exactly. Which regime a real economy is in is an empirical question, not a modelling one.

Over twenty periods the rule does exactly what it claims: the enterprise margin holds up better than under Taylor. Over thirty it has lost, and not because the shock got worse — Taylor's worst reading is identical at both horizons, while Shaikh's keeps falling. That is the balanced path being left, not the shock being absorbed.

The reason is the same insulation that makes the rule work on impact. A fixed interest bill squeezes rE when r falls, which cuts investment back — that is stabilising, and a rule that moves ip with r takes it away. So the mechanism in Result 1 is right, and the policy conclusion does not follow from it. Protecting the enterprise margin and stabilising accumulation are two objectives, and here they conflict.

A pure policy disturbance separates them cleanly: start the policy rate a point high and change nothing else, and both rules bring it back with the same worst-case margin (+0.0118) and the same growth. The disagreement is about distributive shocks, not about the level of rates.

Result 3 · The balanced path is unstable

The baseline is a genuine rest point and not a stable one. The opening figures are snapped to multiples of 1/1024 so the balance sheet cancels exactly in binary, and that 5e−4 nudge is enough to start a departure. It is not numerical noise: departure time scales as 1/kappa, which is what an unstable eigenvalue proportional to kappa looks like.

The loop is the accelerator running through the wage-price spiral. Zeroing either price adjustment or investment response removes it outright; damping wage indexation only delays it. Result 4 · The core on its own, solved

The full model has the mechanism in it and also a wage-price spiral, a demand multiplier, an endogenous profit rate and a Harrod instability. Every experiment measures all of them, so a number like "growth falls 0.4 points" cannot be assigned. A second model has the mechanism and nothing else — two stocks, capital and debt, with the profit rate taken as given. Forty items against 218, and small enough to solve on paper.

In leverage alone it collapses to one equation:

d' = (r − iL·d)(A − κ·d) A = κ − 1 + payF

Two rest points: d* = A/κ, where leverage settles, and d = r/iL, where enterprise profit is nothing. Linearising at the first gives f'(d*) = −κ·rE* — so the growth equilibrium is stable exactly while rE* > 0. That is the claim as a stability condition rather than a simulation.

Result 6 · Corporate equity, and a claim of mine that was wrong

Result 5 said deposits were only 0.47 of the loan book because households hold no equity claim on firms' capital, and that giving them one would raise the deposit base and let more of a policy move through. That was wrong, and rung 1's own arithmetic says so. The bank balance sheet there is an identity, DH = L − EB, and bank capital is pinned by equalisation:

EB/L = (iL − iD − ωB) / (r − iD) = 0.562

A function of the lending rate, the deposit rate, operating costs and r, and of nothing else. No household portfolio enters it. Bank leverage is high here because banks earn a fat net margin — 3.9% of the loan book — and equalisation then demands the capital to match. Getting EB/L to a realistic 0.1 needs iL near 0.040, not a different asset for households to hold.

Measured rather than argued: turning the equity dial from 0 to 0.75 moves DH/L from 0.465 to 0.451. It does essentially nothing, exactly as the identity says.

What corporate equity actually does

Firms meet the gap between what they invest and what they retain by borrowing or by issuing shares. With ψ the share met by issuance, leverage settles at d* = (1 − ψ)(1 − (1−payF)/κ) — so the financing requirement is split, and only the debt half carries an interest bill.

Equity changes the strength of the mechanism. It does not change the transmission.

The two factors of dg/dm = κ·d* × pass-through turn out to be governed by different sectors: firms' financing mix sets the first, banks' funding mix sets the second.

And a policy statement falls out of it. Growth rises with ψ — 0.028 to 0.069 at the baseline margin — because equity carries no interest bill. So equity-financed accumulation is faster and nearly untouchable by policy, and debt-financed accumulation is slower and controllable. The profit-rate-of-enterprise channel is a channel only to the extent that firms borrow.

At ψ = 0 the rung reproduces rung 1 exactly. Capital is financed by debt, outside equity and retained earnings, and K = L + E + RE is integrated from four separate flows and checked rather than enforced — it holds to 7e-13.

Result 7 · The data answers back

Every variable in this model has a national-accounts counterpart, so none of it has to stay theoretical. NIPA table 1.14 splits the net value added of US nonfinancial corporate business into compensation, taxes on production, and net operating surplus — then splits net operating surplus into net interest, transfers, and corporate profits. That is precisely Shaikh's division of pre-interest profit into the creditor's share and enterprise's. The accounts already draw the line the model draws.

B = net interest / net operating surplus the interest burden r = net operating surplus / K rE = (net operating surplus − net interest) / K d = debt securities and loans / K

The capital stock K is Z.1's nonfinancial assets of the sector. B is the reliable one: a ratio of two lines of the same table, with no capital stock in it and nothing to argue about. It is also, not by accident, the quantity the whole argument turns on.

Three verdicts

The constraint has never bound

refuted

The rule proposed from this model was a floor on rE, with rE = 0 — equivalently B = 1 — as a bright line. Against the record: the lowest enterprise profit rate ever recorded is +0.0395, in 1982. Zero of eighty years put even half of pre-interest profit to creditors, let alone all of it. A floor nothing approaches is a floor nobody needs.

The interest deduction carries no information for accumulation

refuted

The model says g = κ(r − iL·d), so the interest bill must enter a regression equal and opposite to the profit rate. That is a testable restriction, and it fails — the bill enters positive in every linear specification. Repairing the obvious objection, that NIPA's net interest understates what is paid, makes it worse rather than better.

One qualification, and it matters. That positive sign is almost certainly simultaneity, not economics — policy tightens into booms, so rates and accumulation rise together, and a reduced form with no instrument for the bill picks that up. So the defensible claim is the weaker one: there is no evidence in the aggregate record for the negative effect the model requires. Establishing one would need a monetary-policy-shock instrument this exercise does not have.

The threshold defence is not refuted — it is untestable here

not settled

The model's claim was never linear. It says a high burden chokes accumulation, and the burden has never been high, so a linear test over this sample could miss a real threshold. Given that chance, the data splits: over the top burden quartile the NIPA bill enters at −1.538 (t −2.33) — the model's sign, and a large effect — while the Baa-imputed bill enters +1.082 over the very same twenty years. Twenty observations cannot tell those apart, and the specification that favours the model rests on the weaker interest measure.

What survives

The profit-driven half of Shaikh is solid. Accumulation follows the profit rate with dg on dr = 0.653, t = 9.4. It is the enterprise half — the interest deduction that makes this model's policy argument possible — that earns nothing in aggregate US data. And even the surviving relation is not stable: regressing g on rE gives a slope of 0.521 (t 5.01) over 1980–99 and 0.078 (t 0.66) over 2000–25. It has essentially disappeared.

The mechanism is real in the model, six times weaker at observed parameters, and has never once bound.

What would revive it

  • Disaggregation. An aggregate burden of 0.17 is entirely consistent with a leveraged tail sitting near 1. Compustat by firm, or Z.1 by size class, would show whether the constraint binds for the marginal borrower while the sector average stays comfortable. This is the likeliest place for the mechanism to be alive.
  • Identification. Romer–Romer or high-frequency policy shocks as an instrument for the interest bill, which would separate the model's negative effect from the simultaneity currently swamping it.

Neither is a reinterpretation of these results. Both are new work, and until one of them lands the honest statement is that the enterprise-margin channel is a coherent piece of theory with no aggregate empirical support.

Result 8 · A different Shaikh: the loan rate as a price of production

Part one fails on the accumulation side. The same chapter carries a second argument, about the interest rate, and it is testable independently — so the calibration failure above does not contaminate it.

Competition within finance equalises rates on a given instrument; competition across sectors sets the level at which financial capital earns the normal profit rate. Impose rB = r on a bank's profit rate and the loan rate falls out as the price of production of banking — unit cost plus normal profit on the capital advanced per dollar lent:

i_N = c + lam * r c operating cost per $ of loans lam capital advanced per $ of loans

Because banks are leveraged, lam < 1 — so i_N < r is derived, not assumed.

That is exactly the joint part one hand-waved. The earlier model simply posited that the lending rate sits below the profit rate; here it follows from banking's balance sheet structure, and with it a positive profit rate of enterprise. In US call reports lam runs 0.016 to 0.130, so the condition holds with enormous room, and i_N < r in 54 of 60 years.

Gibson cannot be tested on this sample and the file says so rather than scoring it. Since 1966 the US price level has been very close to a monotone trend — corr(log p, time) = 0.963 — so regressing a rate on the price level is regressing it on time. The negative coefficients that produces merely record that rates fell after 1981 while prices kept rising. Gibson's correlation was a gold-standard observation, when the price level was itself mean-reverting.

And one deflating decomposition: 93% of i_N is operating cost. The distinctively Shaikhian profit term contributes about 40 basis points of a 5.25% normal rate. The theory's content here is mostly "the loan rate is anchored by what banking costs to run".

Result 9 · Two errors in my own measurement

Both were found while fixing the other, and both were in code I had already committed and reported from.

Summing every row the FDIC returns triple-counts the country

fixed

The summary endpoint keys rows as charter_year_region, and the regions include national aggregates alongside the states. For commercial banks in 2023 the 59 states and territories sum to $11,611bn of net loans — and the endpoint also returns a USA row carrying that same figure, and a US row carrying 11,566. Summing the lot gave $34,836bn, about three times the real loan book. Ratios survived it, numerator and denominator inflating together, which is exactly why nothing looked wrong.

My lam was a quantitative-easing artefact

fixed

Shaikh's lam is fixed capital plus required reserves per dollar of loans. I had used total cash and balances due, which is 91% of the measure and swings from 0.067 in 2007 to 0.322 in 2021 as QE inflates reserve balances at the Fed. Those are voluntary holdings earning interest on reserves, not capital a bank must tie up to lend.

Corrected, lam runs 0.016–0.130 against 0.081–0.359, and has a real trend — 0.127 in 1966–70 down to 0.016 in 2021–25 as reserve requirements were cut and finally abolished outright in March 2020. So lam·r is 40 basis points, not 157.

it cuts both ways

The correction makes the term structure admissible — at the old lam the per-rung profit requirement exceeded the entire observed per-rung step, forcing the implied operating cost negative and making the theory look refuted. But it makes the level fit worse: i_N falls to 5.25% against a 7.79% loan rate. An earlier "inadmissible" verdict was an artefact of the mismeasurement, not a finding about Shaikh.

Result 10 · The recursion counts rungs, not years

Shaikh's term structure makes the shorter rate an input cost to the longer one — the long division funds itself at the short rate — so the curve slopes up on costs, with no expectations and no liquidity premium:

i_m = c_m + i_(m-1) * d_m + lam_m * r

As written that knows nothing about the gap from 1y to 2y being one year and from 20y to 30y being ten. Its fitted d is therefore a function of which maturities happen to be published — 0.35 to 1.02 across ladders, and above 1 on the eight-rung ladder, which is inadmissible. A structural coefficient cannot do that.

Read as a difference equation in maturity it is the Euler step of di/dm = alpha − beta·i, so the funding share must decay with the maturity gap:

Two things the old form could not produce. The spread saturates with maturity, 0.16pp at one year to 0.91pp at twenty-nine, which is the shape a yield curve actually has. And inversion becomes exact:

spread = (i* - i_1) * (1 - exp(-beta * gap))

The curve inverts if and only if the short rate exceeds i* — the long-run price of production of finance.

Which gives an inversion a reading that is not a market forecast: it says policy has driven the short rate above the rate at which banking can reproduce itself.

Result 11 · Four sectors, and where a rate change lands

The three-table model had one hole. Reserves were declared a bank asset and no row ever touched them, because the sector that issues reserves was not in the model — so the three net worths could not sum to zero. They summed to Res.

Adding the public sector closes it. Government's payment medium is the reserve it issues, so it needs no asset column, and every government payment appears in three tables at once. Bonds are split by holder, because a shared stock name is one stock and government's liability cannot be the sum of two holders' assets.

All four net worths now sum to zero — checked on a run, imposed nowhere, holding to 1.8e-13.

The tightening does not destroy financial wealth. It moves it, off the public balance sheet onto all three private ones. On the real side rE falls 0.31pp on impact and 0.61pp by year 40, and the capital stock ends 7.8% lower.

unexpected

One point of tightening roughly doubles banking's profit rate — d rB = +7.4pp on impact. That is arithmetic, not a glitch: lam = 0.05 is 20:1 leverage, and bank assets reprice faster than bank deposits. Equalisation pulls it back afterwards, so the corrective force is visible in the run rather than assumed. The same move that squeezes enterprise by 0.3pp hands banking a windfall an order of magnitude larger in profit-rate terms.

Result 12 · The rule race, and a prediction I got wrong

Anchor policy on the measured normal rate rather than an estimated natural rate, keep the stabilisation terms, and race it against Taylor calibrated to agree at rest. Each rule is scored against its own unshocked path — they do not share one once the model drifts, and comparing shocked levels would charge that divergence to the shock.

On shock response the classical anchor mostly wins, and by more than the two-sector version suggested: it protects the enterprise margin better at 20 and 40 years and loses only by 80, so government debt pushes the crossover out from around 25 years to past 40. It costs less capital and damages the public balance sheet less at every horizon — −1.19 against −2.65 at twenty years, less than half.

But the standing cost is the bigger number

On the unshocked path the classical rule runs permanently tighter, because its anchor follows the normal loan rate up as banking's margin is squeezed while Taylor's fixed intercept does not. That carries a permanently higher interest bill:

burden, year 80, nothing shocked classical 0.0510 Taylor 0.0400 a gap of 1.1 points of output, every year

Against a largest shock-response gap of 0.44 points. The standing cost is about two and a half times the contest the race was set up to measure. A rule anchored on a drifting quantity inherits the drift, and the public finances buy it whether or not anything is shocked.

The fix works, and works too well to be interesting

Make the anchor a trailing average, di1Nbar = mu·(i1N − i1Nbar), so mu dials between the instantaneous classical anchor and a frozen Taylor-style intercept:

Slowing the anchor cuts the standing cost by 86% and protects the margin better and does less fiscal damage and destroys less capital. Every column improves monotonically. There is no tradeoff to price and no interior optimum.

And the prediction that was wrong

I expected a transitory shock to punish a slow anchor — the price-level integral is what the classical rule has that Taylor does not, and a 70-year half-life should waste it. So: knock the wage share one point high and let it revert, half-life 1.7 years.

refuted

It does not discriminate at all. Inflation returns under every rule within about five years, residual gaps of order 1e−5. Worst rE is −0.00044 under all seven settings.

The permanent memory is real in mechanism and negligible in size. A burst of inflation does leave the price level 0.4% high forever, and the anchor does read it through c = p·ucr·tech. But 0.4% on a c of 0.0212 is under a basis point on the normal rate — and the balance-sheet effect of a slightly smaller capital stock is larger, so the surviving rate gap is not merely tiny but the wrong sign, about −2bp.

That qualifies an earlier result rather than overturning it. The two-sector model made the price-level integral look powerful, and it was — but that experiment ran inflation permanently away from the rate at which banking gets cheaper, driving c from 0.049 to between 0.023 and 0.099. A transitory shock moves the price level half a percent and the integral has nothing to bite on. Both results stand; the earlier one was simply not evidence about shocks of this kind, and I read it as though it were.

Where that leaves the rule

  • It beats Taylor on shock response once government debt is in the model — on the enterprise margin, the capital stock and the public balance sheet.
  • Slower is better, with no tradeoff found against either shock type.
  • Its distinctive price-level channel does no measurable work except when inflation is persistently misaligned with the trend in banking costs.

What makes the rule good is not the Gibson mechanism that motivated it. It is that anchoring on a measured cost structure beats a filtered r*.

Which is a narrower claim than the theory wanted, and a more portable one: the distinctively Shaikhian part is doing less work than the merely observable part.

What building it surfaced

Ordered by how much damage each does silently. Every one was measured.

A Godley initial condition is stored at full precision and used at six significant figures

by design

The opening sheet of the four-sector model balanced exactly in Python, every figure an exact multiple of 1/1024, and Minsky's own symbolic row check passed on all four tables. The run still came back out by 8e−5 — and it was constant across 10, 20 and 40 periods, which is the tell: an integration error grows with the horizon and this did not.

The engine writes each initial condition into the .mky twice: once at full precision as the Godley cell text, and once rounded to six significant figures as the value it actually initialises the stock with. On reload the rounded one wins. Both strings are in the saved file — 48.998046875 and 48.998. So a sheet balances at build time, passes the exact != 0 check the engine itself demands, and is still wrong at t=0.

1/1024 is binary-exact but needs ten decimals, so six significant figures destroys it. Snapping to 1/16 satisfies both constraints below 100 and took the residual to 1.8e−13.

An integral shares its output port with its own operation, so wiring drags it instead

fixed

A wire is made by simulating a canvas drag — mouse down on the source's output port, up on the destination's input. An integral's icon and its IntOp both report port 0 at the same pixel, so the drag can land on the operation body and move it rather than start a wire. Every consequence sends you the wrong way: the refusal names overlap, moving the destination 400px away changes nothing because the fault is at the source end, and the only trace is that the IntOp has quietly moved to where the drag dropped it. Whether it happens depends on which icon the canvas picks at that pixel, so the same expression builds in one model and fails in the next.

Several wires into one input combine by the PORT's role, not the operation's name

fixed

Minsky's n-ary operations legitimately take several wires into a single input. The repo's own comment said they sum, which had only ever been tested for add. Measured, with 6 and 3 both wired into port 1:

addsubtractmultiplydivideminmax
99181836
 Port 1 of subtract is the positive side, so it sums; port 1 of divide is the numerator, so it multiplies.

So "they sum their inputs" is wrong for four of the six. Two wires into a multiply give a*b, not a+b, and nothing warns you.

A user function that names a model variable is wrong during a run

fixed

Minsky binds any model variable named in an expression straight to that variable's storage. It reads correctly at reset, then evaluates as 0 for essentially every step of a run. On K' = 0.03·K against the analytic solution: wired blocks were exact to 4e−12; the same model written as 0.03*K inside a function came out 26% low, with the derivative zero on 143 of 144 steps — on both solvers, with no error anywhere.

A user function's arguments live in a different property

fixed

The body is expression; the argument list is parsed out of description. Setting only the body left every function on the x, y a fresh one is born with, so a*b + c was accepted, echoed back verbatim, and computed 0.

Two arguments is the hard limit

fixed

evaluate(in1, in2) zeroes every argument after the second, and the icon only ever grows two input ports. f(a,b,c) = a*100+b*10+c fed 1 and 2 returns 120, not 123.

Reporting a run cost ten times more than solving it

fixed

A solver step on this model costs 1.56 ms. Streaming that step to a client cost 15.4 ms, and the setup before a run cost 2.6 seconds — because live_value_ids(), which walks every item, was being called once per value key from inside a comprehension. And nearly all the cost of reading a variable turned out to be the lookup: all 89 values cost 8.54 ms looked up each step, 0.027 ms through objects bound once per run. Same run, identical result to the last digit: 6.62 s → 0.89 s, which is within a third of the engine's own floor.

Running out of steps looked like finishing

fixed

A truncated run reported a bare "done". It now says where it got to, how many steps the current step size would need, and — when the step size is the giveaway — which solver settings to change. The shipped tolerances take 0.0005-long steps at 45 ms each here: 547 s to reach t = 5, against 3 s explicit, with nothing saying why. The defaults are left alone, because implicit is right for a genuinely stiff model and switching someone's solver silently is not a fix.

There was no way to make a chart

fixed

A model built through the API had nothing to look at — no plot kind existed, so the Plots workspace correctly said "this model has no plots" and offered no way to get one. A plot is also born with room for a single line, so every extra series needed its own chart. The model now ships with three.

Godley rows must balance in exact floating point

by design

The balance check is an exact != 0, no tolerance — and it is right to be. But it means opening figures written at 10 significant digits leave the row out by 1e−08, and at full precision by 7e−15. Only binary-exact values give a clean zero, so the model snaps its opening figures to multiples of 1/1024.

A table's own variable icons cannot be wired

by design

A Godley table draws an icon for every stock and flow, and both are display only. The idiom is to place ordinary copies elsewhere and wire those — in Minsky's own LoanableFunds, every flow has one icon wired into it and several plain copies feeding other blocks. Not discoverable from the outside; it cost an hour.

What held up

  • The accounting. Multi-table, shared stocks, per-column asset classes, symbolic row sums that cancel to zero when the flows match. It held to 1e−15 across every run without a single imposed constraint.
  • The wiring engine is exact. Every ODE built from plain operation blocks integrated to 1e−12 or better against closed forms.
  • Tidy laid out 215 items and 220 wires without overlaps, on a model an order of magnitude larger than any bundled example.

Limits worth knowing

  • Everything measured is an aggregate. The sharpest limit, and the one that decides whether Result 7 is fatal or merely inconvenient. A sector-wide interest burden of 0.17 is consistent with a leveraged tail at which the constraint genuinely binds. Nothing here can see that tail.
  • The interest line is net of interest received. That is the right concept for the model — it is what pre-interest profit is actually reduced by — but it is not a contract rate, and after 2022 it diverges sharply from market yields as firms earned on large balances while their own fixed-rate debt had not repriced. The Baa-imputed gross bill is reported alongside throughout for exactly this reason.
  • The capital stock is at market value (Z.1 nonfinancial assets), so it carries land prices. Ratios like B are immune to this; levels like r and d are not.
  • No instrument, so no causal claim. The regressions in Result 7 are reduced forms. They can say the model's restriction fails; they cannot recover the structural effect of the interest bill on accumulation.
  • The four-sector baseline is not stationary, and shock results are read against the unshocked path because of it. Two reasons, both real: banking's profit rate drifts down as households' deposits outgrow the loan book, and a common growth rate for every financial stock is impossible since dDF = 0 identically — firms hold a constant transaction balance, which is what "firms borrow exactly their gap" means.
  • Household portfolio behaviour is mechanical, and it drives the biggest result. Bonds are bought pro rata out of the deficit with no sensitivity to return. The deposit growth that squeezes banking's margin — and hence the standing cost that dominates Result 12 — is a consequence of that rule. A return-sensitive household would change the size of that cost, though not its existence.
  • The term structure is fitted to Treasuries, not to bank loan rates. Shaikh's theory is about loans; he argues arbitrage carries the shape across, and this leans on that.
  • Utilisation is unbounded above. Output is demand-determined with no capacity ceiling.
  • No default, no credit rationing, no bank failure. Banking's profit rate can halve without anything breaking.
  • One household sector — workers and rentiers are not separated, so there is no differential saving propensity, which Shaikh would want, and which would matter most for exactly the redistribution Result 11 measures.
