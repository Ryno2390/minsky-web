Marx Volume II · US national accounts 1925–2026 · twenty-three tests

The Wrong Half

An investigation that set out to explain the productivity slowdown, eliminated every explanation running through capital, localised the residual precisely, failed to explain it — and then found that the thing it was really trying to account for was somewhere else entirely.

Built in MinskyBEA · BLS · Z.1 · FRED · SEC · NBER-CESTwenty-three modulesThirteen corrections against itself

What this was for

Americans report, poll after poll and for decades, that they are getting worse off. The aggregates say the opposite: real GDP per capita has roughly doubled since 1984. One of those is usually treated as a misperception.

Neither is. This began as an attempt to explain the productivity slowdown — the standard candidate for a felt decline — and worked through it properly: Marx's reproduction schemes built as a running model, then the circuit of capital, the composition of investment, obsolescence, offshoring, the return to research. Then, when that turned out to be the wrong half, down through prices and distribution to what people are actually holding.

The productivity slowdown is real and mostly unexplained. It is also, it turns out, not what people are feeling. The last two tests found the felt decline sitting in two numbers that need none of the preceding work, and the whole sequence is worth reading as the elimination that establishes that.

Part one · the model

Marx's reproduction schemes, as something that runs

Volume II writes each department's year as c + v + s and asks what has to be true for the whole thing to repeat. Netting out what each department buys from itself, the answer is one condition — and written as money flows across four sectors, that condition turns out to be exactly the requirement that Department I's cash holding not drain.

simple II c = I (v + s) 2000 = 2000 expanded II (c + dc) = I (v + dv + s consumed) 1600 = 1600

The reproduction condition is the stationarity of a money stock, which is the object a Godley table exists to track. So the model imposes it nowhere: four sectors, money as the only asset, no credit — Volume II Part III's own abstraction — and the condition is left to hold or fail.

It holds. Simple reproduction runs stationary at 9800 with every money stock flat and the condition at exactly zero. Expanded runs at precisely the predicted 10% growth with the condition at 4×10⁻¹⁶ of Dept I's product and money conserved to 10⁻¹¹ over ten years. The recursion reproduces Marx's published table — 9000, 9800, 10780, 11858, 13043.8, 14348.18 — to four decimals.

And it is a knife edge

Dept II's accumulation rate is not a decision: α₂ = α₁(1+q₂)/(1+q₁) = 0.300 exactly. There is a second condition on the opening proportions, c₂/v₁ = 16/11, which Marx's own first year violates — which is why his scheme grows 8.89% in year one and exactly 10% ever after. Both must hold. With only the accumulation rate right, the residual sits at a constant −0.83%. With both, it is 2×10⁻¹⁴.

Give Dept II its own rate and the failure has a mechanism the arithmetic cannot show: the short department does not discover its error through unsold goods. It discovers it by running out of cash. Five points off the balanced rate and Firms I cannot pay wages by year nine.

found the hard way

Solving for the opening capital directly gives 16000/11, which does not terminate in binary, gets snapped to 1454.5625 by the Godley grid, and lands the model 10⁻⁵ off a fixed point that has no neighbourhood. The residual looked like solver error until checked. Marx's own year two — 4400c + 1100v, 1600c + 800v — is on the path and is all integers.

Part two · the eliminations

Every explanation that runs through capital

Turnover time is what Volume II spends two Parts on and Part III assumes away. Marx's ch.16 result — two identical capitals returning 1000% and 100% a year on different turnover — generalises the balance conditions rather than breaking them, and produces a genuine bound: g ≤ α·e·n_agg, never violated over 200,000 random capitals. A slow-turning economy cannot grow fast.

So: did the circuit slow?

137.8 → 125.6cash cycle, days 17.9 → 12.3fixed capital life, years 111 → 89inventory days

Both legs of the circuit got faster, which is why there is no divergence to draw here. Depreciation is 15.4% of value added against 9.4% in the fifties. Nothing measured in physical time got slower.

And the profit rate on capital advanced recovered: 9.08% in 1955–69, a trough of 6.52% in the eighties, 8.93% now — within two percent of where it started. The one measure that falls, the output-capital ratio, turns out to be the price of land and buildings: redone against equipment at current cost, where no revaluation is possible, it rises.

The wedge is real, and the channel is not

Gross investment rose from 10.23% of GDP to 13.51% while net investment fell from 3.39% to 2.57%. Four fifths of gross investment now replaces what wore out, against two thirds in the fifties. Intellectual property alone accounts for 115% of the rise in the depreciation rate — it depreciates 8.7 times faster than structures, a 4.1-year life against 35.9.

10.2 → 13.5%gross investment / GDP 3.4 → 2.6%net investment / GDP

The maintenance proportion went 66.9% → 81.0%. The wedge is exactly as described — and then the channel it was supposed to act through does not appear.

Capital deepening is net investment over the stock less employment growth. Net investment fell by a third; employment growth fell by half. They very nearly cancel, and deepening ends at 1.09% a year against 1.08% in the fifties. Flat, not halved. Less net investment did not mean less capital per worker.

Obsolescence cannot bite where wear already has

The relative price of equipment fell 72% since 1947, and BEA quality-adjusts, so that is new capital getting better per dollar. But an age-a machine costs e^((γ+δ)a) times the frontier to run: obsolescence and wear enter only as their sum, and they are substitutes in the replacement decision. In the 1950s obsolescence was 1–8% of total decay. Even a tenfold rise in the rate of progress moves the optimal life by single digits — model −7% to −24%, recorded −6.3%.

A machine that wears out in eight years cannot be made obsolete much faster than that. Moral depreciation can only bite on capital that would otherwise have lasted a long time.

Which is why obsolescence shows up not as shorter lives within a class but as a shift of investment into classes short-lived by nature. Capital did not get replaced faster. It changed into a kind that has to be.

And it was not offshoring

63% of the shift into intellectual property had happened before China joined the WTO. The fastest decade for the IP share is 1982–92; for imports, 1998–2008; for manufacturing employment loss, 1966–76. Three different decades is hard for one causal chain.

Deflated to 1960 = 100, real equipment capital stands at 595, structures 721, IP 2151 — against manufacturing employment at 83. Every class of capital grew several-fold including the one offshoring supposedly removed. BEA's industry detail then settles it: manufacturing did transform hardest, its IP share going 13.2% → 35.4%, but non-manufacturing carries 69% of the economy-wide shift — publishing, data processing, telecoms, insurance, legal services. Industries with no production to send anywhere.

The machinery stayed and the jobs went. That is automation, not exit.

Part three · the residual

Localised precisely, explained hardly at all

With capital eliminated, the slowdown is in total factor productivity by construction — capital's contribution to labour productivity was 0.59 points in 1950–69 and 0.88 in 2020–24. It never slowed. TFP growth fell from 1.91% to 0.93%.

Mismeasurement cannot carry it either, and this one can be sized. Had TFP kept its 1950–69 pace, output would be 65% higher today. For mismeasured output to be the story that entire 65% must be product the accounts miss: $19tn a year on a $29tn economy. Confine it to the fifth of the economy plausibly hard to measure and that fifth must be understated by 326%.

What is measurable is the arithmetic of short-lived assets. Productivity accounting weights capital by its rental price, not its dollar value, so a dollar of IP depreciating at 24% carries about four times the weight of a dollar of structures at 2.8%. IP is now a third of capital by rental weight against a seventh by value, and capital services grow faster than the stock: the gap widens from +0.75 to +1.62 points, which at a capital share of 0.33 subtracts 0.19 points from measured TFP. About a fifth of the slowdown, robust from r = 2% to 8%.

The fact everything converges on

×26real R&D capital ÷45return per unit

Research capital is 26 times its 1950s level and buys 57% of the TFP growth. Measured independently at industry level over sixty years: research-intensive manufacturing grew TFP 1.09 points a year faster than non-research manufacturing in the 1960s and 0.37 points slower by 2010–16. The advantage did not shrink. It inverted.

That result was reached from BEA capital stocks, and it agrees closely with the spending-based literature that got there from R&D budgets and researcher headcount. Two measurements built for different purposes, landing in the same place.

One candidate that made a prediction

The most specific explanation on offer is that know-how shifted from non-excludable to excludable, because the vertically integrated firms that published research broke up. Its structural premise is confirmed and large: in 1960, 92% of America's research capital sat inside manufacturing firms, and the industries holding research had 82% of their own capital in plant. Today those are 57% and 53%.

It also makes the one prediction that separates it from "the ideas got harder to find": the private return should hold up while the social return collapses. Fishing-out predicts both fall together.

the two returns1950s–60snowverdict
private, firm accounts—27.4% vs a 19% user costclears
social, TFP per unit of research1.000.022÷45
 If the well were simply dry, firms would not be clearing their cost of capital on research either. They are.
correction · the largest in this investigation

Rising concentration of R&D profits was reported as the strongest evidence, and specifically as a trend that escaped the objection that the wedge was always there. It does not escape it. Drop the five largest earners — Apple, Alphabet, Microsoft, Nvidia, Meta, who take 43.8% of all operating income among research-doing filers — and the rise vanishes: the Herfindahl is 0.0108 in 2010 and 0.0098 in 2024. What survives is a level difference, which is a weaker and different claim.

The sixty-year industry test then declined to confirm capture at all: net profit rates rose ~16% in the most research-intensive industries and ~14% in the least. The thesis survives only in its dissipation form — excludability makes research a race, races burn resources, and the rents are competed away rather than kept. That version predicts exactly the absence of differential capture that the data show.

So the residual is localised and not explained. Three mechanisms fit and cannot be separated: the frontier is further from any starting researcher; racing dissipates what excludability creates; and research aimed at firm-specific advantage has a smaller blast radius than research that gets published. TFP measures blast radius.

Part four · the wrong half

What people are actually feeling

Two tests were meant to finish the productivity story and instead ended it.

The first asked whether research had gone to unproductive uses in Marx's sense — to circulation rather than production. Half right: the productive share of R&D fell from 92.5% to somewhere between 59% and 85%, and finance and insurance went from doing essentially no research to 7.6% of the total. But the outcome Marx's argument is actually about went the other way.

×12services prices since 1960 −32%durables since their 1995 peak

Commodity production did exactly what Marx said productive labour does. It cheapened commodities, relentlessly, and has not stopped. Durable goods prices peaked in 1995 and have fallen 32% in nominal terms since.

Which invites Baumol — the weight moved onto a sector where productivity grows slowly. Measured properly, holding manufacturing's employment share at its 1960s level recovers 0.31 points of a 1.20-point slowdown. About a quarter. The other three quarters is not reallocation but the sectors themselves slowing, and Baumol's services grow slowly and constantly. A constant drag cannot produce an accelerating one.

And then the demographic adjustment, which pointed somewhere else

Capital per head of population — counting retirees, children, everyone who consumes without producing — grew in every decade since 1960 and grew faster than capital per worker in six of seven. The share of adults working is higher now than in 1960, not lower. But:

The living-standards slowdown is roughly twice the productivity slowdown, and half of it was never about production at all.

The diagnosis

Two measured things produce the feeling, and neither needs the productivity puzzle solved.

Fifty percent is not extreme. National accounts put housing near 18% of consumption, healthcare 17%, education 3% — some 38% before childcare, and higher for a household raising children in a city. The anchor share also rises as income falls, because rent and medicine do not scale down. The lower rows are not a worst case; they are where most of the distribution sits.

The CPI is not wrong. It is answering a different question from the one people are asking when they say they are worse off. Income rose against a basket dominated by what got cheap and fell against what cannot be avoided.

A productivity slowdown is why the pie grew more slowly. It is not why most people got a smaller slice of a pie that still doubled.

Part five · following it down

Why the anchors ran away

Goods are inputs to shelter, medicine and schooling, so cheaper goods should have pulled their prices down. The control that answers this comes before any of the popular explanations: these are labour-intensive services, so the benchmark is not the price of a commodity but the price of an hour.

 Against an hour of compensation, shelter is cheaper than in 1984. Tuition is 79% dearer. The four anchors are not one phenomenon, and whatever explains one cannot be what explains another.

Four accounts get tested one at a time. Zoning survives, and by the quantity rather than the price: housing starts per thousand people fell from 8.17 in the 1970s to 3.10 in the 2010s. The flow price tracked wages because rent is anchored to a large existing stock; the constraint lands on the price of getting in.

3.99 → 5.00house price / income, years 100 → 109shelter rent vs wages

A supply constraint on new building does exactly this. The story is right and the price index is the wrong instrument for seeing it.

The others fare worse against timing. The whole of medical's excess over wages is 1984 to 2000 — 148 then, 149 now — while hospital bargaining is told as a recent development. Tuition's excess is front-loaded into 1984–1994, before Direct Lending or Grad PLUS existed, and reverses 10% in the decade of the largest student-loan balances. Female participation, the childcare demand story, rose 22 points to 2000 and has fallen since while the price kept climbing.

But one counterfactual outranks all four of them.

 A median household growing at the country's own rate would have outrun shelter, medicine and schooling by 13% at exactly the prices charged. It came in 21% short. The anchors are unaffordable because they beat the median, and the economy beat the median by more.

Distribution of what, exactly

Three quite different things sit inside that 30-point gap, and only two are distributional.

 Real median personal income is up 61% since 1984 against the household measure's 39%. Nobody took that 22-point difference from anyone — households got smaller. On a per-person basis the median falls 8% short of the anchor bundle rather than 21%.

The labour share did fall — the wage share of gross domestic income went 46.2% to 42.7%, the nonfarm labour share index down 10.7% — and it is the smallest of the three. And one finding cuts against the framing entirely: median full-time weekly earnings rose to 117.9 against production wages at 117.6. The middle did not fall behind the typical worker. Whatever opened, opened between the top and everyone else.

Not chief executives, and the refutation is arithmetic

The divergence is worth about $2.09tn a year, 7.1% of GDP. Set that against every chief executive in the country, drawn as generously as the facts allow:

definitiontotal payshare of the gap
S&P 500 chief executives at $16m each$8.0bn 0.38%
every public-company CEO at $5m each$20.0bn 0.96%
every BLS "chief executive" at $250k $50.0bn2.40%
 Two orders of magnitude too small. Divide the divergence by candidate group sizes and only groups in the millions give a per-person figure that is a salary rather than a fantasy: $4.2bn each at 500 CEOs, $131,000 at the top 10%, $30,000 across all management and professional occupations.

Which resolves an apparent contradiction rather than creating one. Wealth concentrates through asset prices with no payroll behind it — the top 0.1% wealth share rose 62% since 1989 against the top 1%'s 34%, and inside the top 1% the top tenth of it gained share too. Income has to be paid, to countable people, out of a bill of known size. That constraint is why the income side needs millions and the wealth side does not.

two corrections, one file apart

The payroll aggregate that produced "the supervisory premium doubled" is a group mean, and a mean rises identically whether everyone gained or a handful gained enormously. The same 20% is produced by a fifth of workers paid twice as much, by 1% paid five times as much, or by 0.1% paid fifteen times. It was never evidence for the fifth.

And "composition is zero" was true only of the classification used. The establishment survey's supervisory share fell 19.2% to 18.6%; the household survey's occupational classification has management and professional occupations going 33.8% to 43.8% of employment — twenty-four million more people. A nurse is professional and nonsupervisory, so both are true, but a material part of the gap is headcount after all.

A gap that opened once

The natural name for what is left is the rise of a professional, credentialed and managerial class above everyone else. The name is fair. The tense is wrong.

+18.6%divergence 1984–2000 +4.2%divergence 2000–2024

Four fifths of forty years of divergence happened in the first sixteen. The dashed line is the professional share of employment, which kept climbing throughout — 29.0% to 43.8%.

And the credential premium stopped rising. Median weekly earnings for a bachelor's degree and above over high-school-only: 1.63× in 2000 and 1.65× in 2024. Flat across a quarter century. Holding the premium frozen and letting only the share move separates the two halves cleanly:

periodshare movescomposition gives measuredexplained
1984–200029.0 → 33.8%+2.7% +18.6%14%

 Before 2000, six sevenths of the divergence is the professional class being paid relatively more. After 2000 the share shift alone over-predicts, so the premium slightly compressed while the class grew.

So it is the rise of a professional class, largely completed by 2000, followed by a quarter century of that class getting bigger rather than richer. Its share of employment went 29.0% to 43.8%. A class containing nearly half the workforce is not a class in the sense the phrase implies, and "above the rest" now describes a majority standing above a minority.

Most of what people feel now is not a process still running. It is a gap that opened once, was never closed, and is measured against a memory of the years before it opened.

That may be the most useful thing here for explaining the polling. A static gap generates persistent grievance in a way an actively widening one does not, because there is no year in which things got better — only a level, held, for a quarter of a century.

What the sequence establishes

  • The productive sector still works. The circuit is faster than in 1955, the profit rate on capital advanced recovered to within two percent of its 1950s level, capital deepening never slowed, and durable goods have fallen 32% in nominal price since 1995. Nothing went wrong in making things.
  • Every quantity-of-capital explanation is closed. Not less investment, not slower turnover, not offshoring, not a thinner stock. The slowdown is in TFP by construction.
  • A fifth of the TFP slowdown is measured arithmetic — short-lived assets carrying more rental weight — and mismeasurement is bounded small by requiring $19tn a year of unrecorded output.
  • The rest is a collapse in the return to research, measured two independent ways, with three candidate mechanisms and no way here to separate them.
  • And none of that is what is being felt. The felt decline is distribution and the relative price of shelter, care and schooling — measurable in an afternoon, and actionable without solving the first.
  • The anchors are wages, not goods. Against an hour of compensation shelter is cheaper than in 1984. Tuition is 79% dearer. Of four popular explanations only housing supply survives its timing test, and a median household growing at GDP per capita would have outrun the whole bundle by 13%.
  • It is distribution, and a third of it is not. Household size accounts for about eleven of the thirty points. Labour's share of income is the smallest real term. The largest sits inside labour.
  • It is not chief executives. Every one of them is 2.4% of a $2.09tn divergence. The income side requires millions of people; the wealth side does not, because asset prices need no payroll.
  • And it stopped in 2000. Four fifths of the divergence opened between 1984 and 2000, when the professional premium was rising. Since then the premium is flat and the class has grown instead — from 29% of employment to 44%.

What would move the open question

Research duplication data — rates of simultaneous invention, patent interference, overlapping trials — would test the racing mechanism directly, and nothing here measures it. Compustat firm-level over sixty years would supply the within-industry dispersion that industry averages are blind to by construction. Industry TFP outside manufacturing before 2005 would close Baumol properly. In that order.

Thirteen corrections, kept in place

Nearly every appealing result in this investigation weakened when tested, and the corrections are left in the source rather than quietly folded in, because the pattern is the most useful output.

  • A counterexample that did not exist. A constructed case where faster turnover went with slower growth — in that same table the faster-turning economy grew faster, and all four cases were monotone. Chasing why produced the real result: the bound g ≤ α·e·n_agg, which cannot be violated.
  • A ceiling with no content. That bound was then used as a diagnostic. Its slack is exactly c/(c+v) — the wage share of costs wearing a different hat, identical to every decimal across seven eras.
  • An empty parse, cached. BEA writes year headers as text, an isinstance check matched none of them, and the empty result was written to cache where it would have been served forever. Second time in this repo.
  • A baseline that was mostly Hollywood. Research effort was measured with BEA's intellectual-property total, which in 1950 was 69% films, television, books and music. The correction strengthened the result, which is why it went unnoticed.
  • Five companies. Described above.
  • A units convention that inverted an answer. Gross-output TFP against a value-added aggregate makes manufacturing look like the lagging sector and refutes Baumol on the first line. Converted, it leads.
  • Sampling at the zeros. A replacement-cycle test sampled years 5, 10, 15, 30 against a ten-year asset life — exactly where the oscillation passes through zero — so two rows read flat while swinging by half the stock.
  • A panel truncated by one series. Manufacturing value added starts in 2005 on FRED and sat inside an intersection, silently cutting a sixty-year timing test to nineteen years.
  • Deepening that "roughly halved" while its own table showed it ending where it started.
  • A household measure read as a personal one. The anchor-squeeze finding used median household income, and households got smaller. Per person the median falls 8% short of the anchor bundle, not 21%.
  • Benefits counted as inequality. The series showing the top pulling away included employer benefits on one side and not the other. Worth 3 of the 24 points — the finding survived a check it should have had first.
  • A group mean read as a group. "The supervisory fifth is paid twice as much" was never what the number said: the identical aggregate is produced by 0.1% of workers paid fifteen times as much.
  • "No composition effect" from one classification. True of the establishment survey's supervisory flag, false of the household survey's occupations, which show twenty-four million more people in professional work.
